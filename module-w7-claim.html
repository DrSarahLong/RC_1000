RC 1000 · Week 7 · Module

From Experience to a Claim

New project. In the personal essay you told a true story. Now you'll make an argument, one you can defend, that grows out of something you've actually lived. Same honesty, new job: taking a position.

Week 1 of the argument project. This week you land on what you're arguing.

A quick orientation

An argument grounded in the personal often starts from something that cost you something, an unfair rule, a belief you've had to push back on, a system that failed someone you love. That's allowed to have feeling in it. You choose the ground you stand on, and you can pick a lower-stakes topic if you'd rather. There's no wrong choice here.

Part 1 · Learn

What makes something an argument

An , in writing, isn't a fight. It's a : a position you take and then defend with reasons and evidence. The trick is telling a real claim apart from the things that look like one:

  • A topic is just a subject. "School start times." It says nothing yet.
  • A fact can be looked up and settled. "My high school started at 7:20 a.m." No one can argue it.
  • A claim is a position a reasonable person could with. "High schools should start later." Now you have something to defend.

And here's the move that separates a strong claim from a shaky one: precision. A claim can fail two opposite ways, too timid to matter, or so sweeping you can't possibly back it up. The strong version sits between them, specific and carefully .

Topic → claim:

Just a topicThis essay is about phones in school.
A claim you can defendMy high school should have kept phones in lockers during class, because the year I kept mine on the desk "just in case," I lost more focus than I ever noticed I was spending.

Overclaimed → qualified:

OverclaimedPhones have completely destroyed this generation's ability to think.
Qualified, and strongerFor students like me who treated a phone as a safety blanket, the constant checking quietly cost us focus we didn't know we had.

The qualified version is easier to defend, not weaker. "Completely destroyed" invites one counterexample to sink it. "Students like me who…" claims exactly as much as your evidence can carry.

Why arguing the other side makes you stronger

It feels backward, but seeking out the best case against your claim sharpens it. In a classic study, Lord, Lepper, and Preston (1984) found that asking people to "" cut the bias in their reasoning. For you, that means a claim you've already stress-tested against its strongest objection is one a reader can trust. We'll build that habit in this week's writing.

Grounded in the personal.

Your lived experience is where this argument starts, and it's real evidence: you were there. Later in the project you'll add outside sources to widen the case beyond you. But the heat that makes an argument worth reading usually comes from a writer who has skin in it.

Not the academic paper you're picturing

When people hear "argumentative essay," they picture the formal : third person, no "I," a thesis stated flatly up top, evidence stacked in tidy paragraphs, a cool and distant voice. That form is real and useful, and you'll write it in courses like RC 2001. Ours is a different animal. We're writing argument that borrows the tools of , scene, concrete detail, voice, and your own "I" as evidence and authority. The rigor of a real, defensible claim, carried by the craft of story.

Standard academic version. "Research indicates that later school start times correlate with improved adolescent performance (Wahlstrom, 2014). This paper argues that districts should adjust their schedules accordingly."
Ours, the same claim built with craft. "I have slept through more first periods than I can count. It wasn't laziness. I was sixteen, it was 7 a.m., and my body simply was not there yet. That is the case I want to make: my district should start high school later, and I will show you what those lost mornings cost."

Both make the identical claim. The second uses a scene, a voice, and a real person to make you feel the stakes before it reasons about them. That's the move this project teaches: argument's rigor, told with a writer's craft.

Part 3 · Practice

The claim clinic

For each statement, decide which it is: a topic or fact (nothing to argue), an overclaim (too sweeping to defend), or a defensible claim (a position, sized to its evidence). Commit, then Check.

"Social media is bad for teenagers."

Overclaim. "Bad," "teenagers," all of it, is too broad to defend. Which platform, which harm, for whom? Narrow it and it becomes arguable.

"My old high school started first period at 7:20 a.m."

A fact. It can be checked and settled, so there's nothing to argue. It could become the evidence under a claim, but it isn't one.

"My district should push high school start times later, because the year I spent asleep in first period taught me a 7 a.m. teenager isn't learning, just attending."

A defensible claim. It takes a position, gives a reason, and grounds it in lived experience, sized to what one writer can genuinely back.

"Everyone would be happier if they just deleted their phones."

Overclaim. "Everyone," "just," and "happier" promise far more than any evidence could deliver. One happy phone user disproves it.

Argument

In academic writing, an argument isn't a quarrel; it's a claim supported by reasons and evidence. The goal isn't to "win" or to be loudest, it's to make a case a fair-minded reader could follow and find reasonable, even if they start out disagreeing. Feeling is welcome; it just can't do the work of evidence. For example, "my campus should keep 24-hour library access" becomes an argument once you back it with reasons (students who work days can only study at night) and evidence, rather than just asserting it.

Claim

The position your whole argument defends, the answer to "so what are you actually saying should be true?" A good claim is debatable (someone could disagree), specific (not vague), and sized to your evidence (you can actually back it up). Everything else in the essay exists to support it. For example, "the dining hall should stay open past 8" is a claim; "the dining hall" by itself is only a topic.

Debatable

The test for whether you have a claim: could a reasonable person disagree? "The library closes at 10" isn't debatable (it's a fact). "The library should stay open until midnight" is, so it can anchor an argument. If no one could disagree, you have a topic or a fact, not a claim yet.

Overclaiming

Making a claim bigger than your evidence can support, usually with absolute words: always, never, everyone, completely, proves, destroys. Overclaiming feels bold but is actually fragile: a single counterexample knocks it over. The fix is to qualify, claim exactly as much as you can defend, and no more. For example, "phones destroyed our generation" overclaims; "the phone on my desk quietly cost me focus I didn't notice I was spending" claims only what you can show.

Qualifying a claim

Adding the truthful limits to a claim so it fits your evidence: often, in many cases, for students like me, in schools like mine, tends to. The philosopher Stephen Toulmin called this the "qualifier," and it's a mark of a careful thinker, not a weak one. A qualified claim is harder to knock down because it never claimed more than it could hold.

Consider the opposite

A reasoning habit studied by Lord, Lepper & Preston (1984): before you commit to a position, deliberately build the best case against it. Their subjects reasoned more fairly when asked to do this. For a writer, it does two things: it shows you where your claim is weak (so you can fix it), and it lets you answer objections before a reader raises them. For example, before arguing your town should ban gas leaf-blowers, spend five minutes building the landscaper's case, then write to answer it.

The academic argument

The formal argumentative essay taught in most research and disciplinary writing: an impersonal, often third-person case that states its thesis up front and defends it with sources and data, keeping the writer's own voice in the background. Example opening: "Studies indicate that food deserts contribute to poorer health outcomes; this paper argues that municipal zoning should incentivize grocery access." Rigorous and useful, and the main event in a course like RC 2001. Here we borrow its rigor without its distance.

Creative nonfiction (CNF)

True writing that uses the techniques of fiction, scene, character, concrete sensory detail, voice, and narrative shape, to make real experience vivid on the page. A personal essay is creative nonfiction; so is a memoir or a literary op-ed. Example: instead of "the clinic was underfunded," a CNF writer shows "the waiting room had three chairs and a sign-in sheet on a clipboard chained to the wall." This project aims those tools at an argument.
