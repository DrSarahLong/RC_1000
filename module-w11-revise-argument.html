RC 1000 · Week 11 · Module

Revise the Argument

You have a full draft with sources. This week you make the case tighter and fairer, check that every source earns its place, and make sure you still sound like you. Then you turn it in.

Final week of the argument project. Revise this week; submit Sunday.

Before you start

Revising an argument you believe in can feel like poking holes in your own side. That's the job, and it's a strength. The version that has survived your own toughest questions is the one a reader will trust.

Part 1 · Learn

Revising an argument is its own kind of re-seeing

In Project 1 you revised for scene and story. An argument asks different questions. You still cut what doesn't serve the whole, but now "the whole" is your , and everything gets measured against it: does this paragraph advance the case? Does this reason have evidence? Have I been fair to the other side? Six things to check:

  • The claim holds and is . Still defensible, still sized to your evidence, no sneaky overclaiming.
  • Structure builds. Each paragraph moves the argument forward. A makes a weak order obvious.
  • Evidence backs every reason. No claim left hanging with only your say-so where a source should be.
  • Sources are , not dumped. Every quote framed and explained, in your voice.
  • The is answered. You name the strongest objection and respond to it.
  • You still sound like you. The craft, voice, a real person, hasn't been buried under citations.

Here's the same paragraph before and after that kind of revision:

Before (overclaimed, quote dumped, one-sided)Phones have completely destroyed everyone's focus. "Task-switching lowers retention" (Lee 4). Schools should ban them, period.
After (qualified, sandwiched, answers the objection)I know the worry: for a lot of parents, a phone is an emergency lifeline, and that fear is real. But for students like me who kept one on the desk "just in case," the cost was quieter and constant. As Lee found, "even brief task-switching measurably lowers retention" (4). That was every class of mine, present, but never quite there.

Same claim, far stronger: it qualifies ("students like me"), frames its source, and answers the other side before moving on, all without losing the voice.

Part 3 · Practice

Diagnose the weak argument

For each excerpt, pick what most needs fixing. Commit, then Check, the feedback names the move and the fix.

"My town should fund the youth center. "Programs like these cut juvenile arrests by a third" (Reyes 2). It would really help."

Quote dumped. The statistic is strong but sits naked between two vague sentences. Frame it (who is Reyes?) and explain what it means for your town.

"Everyone knows online classes are a total failure that ruins every student's education, no exceptions."

Overclaimed. "Everyone," "total failure," "every student," "no exceptions", one counterexample sinks it. Qualify it to what you can actually defend.

"Later start times are obviously right and there's no good reason anyone could have against them. Moving on."

Ignores the counterargument. There are real objections (bus routes, jobs, childcare). Naming and answering them makes you more convincing, not less.

"Schools should offer free breakfast. Anyway, my little brother is a really picky eater and once cried over scrambled eggs for an hour."

Off topic. The eggs story is charming but doesn't advance the claim about free breakfast. In an argument, cut what doesn't serve the case, even the fun bits.

Part 4 · Do

Revise, then submit

This isn't a checklist you tick. Two tools make you actually revise by marking up your own draft, so you see what's really there instead of assuming it. Both run on your computer, and both let you download what you find into your notebook.

1. Structure. Open the Reverse-Outline X-Ray: paste your draft and label each paragraph's job. A weak order, a repeat, or a paragraph that doesn't advance the claim will jump out.

2. The case itself. Open the Revision X-Ray: highlight your claim, your evidence, and your counterargument in different colors. If you can't find one of them in your own draft, that is exactly what to fix. Then check that every source is actually cited, and hear the whole thing read back.

Reverse-Outline X-Ray → Revision X-Ray →

Then two quick passes by hand: make sure every source is (lead-in, cited, explained) rather than dumped, re-check for the Week 8 fallacies (straw man, false dilemma, hasty generalization), and tidy your citations with the MLA Citation Builder.

That's a real argument, built and defended. A claim grounded in your own life, widened with evidence, made fair, and written in your voice. That's a genuinely hard thing to do well, and you did it. Claim (recap)

The position your whole argument defends. In revision, the test is whether every paragraph serves it. Example: if a paragraph is interesting but doesn't help prove your claim, it either needs to connect back or get cut. The claim is the ruler you measure everything else against.

Qualified (recap)

A claim limited to what your evidence can support, using words like "for students like me," "often," "in cases like this." Example: "phones ruin focus" is overclaimed; "for students who keep a phone on the desk, the constant checking costs focus" is qualified, and much harder to knock down. In revision, hunt for absolute words (all, every, never, completely) and pull them back.

Reverse outline

An outline you make after drafting: go paragraph by paragraph and write, in one line, the job each does. Example: "1: hooks with my story. 2: states claim. 3: same point as 2 (cut?). 4: evidence." Laid out together, a repeated or out-of-order paragraph jumps out. The Reverse-Outline X-Ray tool does this with your pasted draft.

Quote sandwich (recap)

The three-layer way to bring in a source: a lead-in, the cited quote or paraphrase, then your explanation. Example: "As Reyes reports (lead-in), 'programs like these cut juvenile arrests by a third' (2) (filling). For a town our size, that's dozens of kids a year (explanation)." In revision, find any quote sitting alone and build the two slices around it.

Counterargument

The strongest objection to your claim, raised and answered inside your own essay. Addressing it makes you more persuasive, because it shows you've thought it through and aren't hiding the hard part. Example: arguing for later start times, you admit the real problem of bus schedules, then explain why the trade is still worth it. Ignoring the objection just leaves the reader thinking it.
