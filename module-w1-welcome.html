RC 1000 · Week 1 · Module

Welcome: Writing to Discover

You've written your whole life, texts, captions, essays you didn't love. This class is about writing that's actually yours, and getting real at it. First, let's get set up and clear out some myths.

Weeks 1 and 2 lay the foundation. Take your time; a couple of sittings is plenty.

A note before we start

This is an online class, but it isn't a lonely one, I'm here, and I read everything you turn in and give you real feedback. You don't have to be a "good writer" to do well here. You have to be willing to write, mess up, and revise. If you can do that, you're exactly where you belong.

Part 1 · Learn

What this class believes about writing

Most people carry around a few ideas about writing that quietly hold them back, that good writers get it right the first time, that you're either born with it or not, that you should know what you'll say before you start. All false. This course runs on the opposite:

  • Writing is . You write to find out what you think, not to type up something you already finished in your head.
  • Writing is . It's a skill, like an instrument. It answers to practice and feedback, both of which you'll get here.
  • Writing is . Even personal writing makes choices for someone else. It's a social, rhetorical act, not private venting.
And here's a bonus the research keeps confirming

The act of writing doesn't just show your thinking, it improves it. Studies find that getting worries and half-formed thoughts out of your head and onto the page frees up mental space and sharpens how you think (Ramirez & Beilock even found students who before a test did better on it). We'll use writing as a thinking tool all semester.

Part 4 · Do

Start writing

Your Writer's Notebook. All semester you'll keep a running notebook of low-stakes writing, freewrites, notes, reactions. It's where ideas start. It saves on your own computer as you type, and it's private unless you choose to share a piece. You turn the whole notebook in just twice all semester, once at midterm and once at the end, as a completion check: it isn't graded on quality, the point is simply that you did the thinking. The two writes below begin it.

1. What matters to you (about 10 minutes). Pick one thing you genuinely value, family, a faith, creativity, fairness, your friends, a place, being the first in your family at college, anything real. Write about why it matters to you and a specific time it mattered. Don't argue; just tell the truth.

This isn't a warm-up for its own sake. Researchers (Cohen & Sherman) find that measurably helps students feel they belong and perform better, especially students who worry they don't fit. Take it seriously; it's for you.

Private, saves on your own computer only. Saved ✓

2. Your first freewrite (about 10 minutes). means writing without stopping and without judging, keep your fingers moving even if you write "I don't know what to say." No one grades this; no one even reads it unless you want them to. Write about your history with writing: a time it went well, a time it went badly, how you feel picking it up now.

Private, saves on your own computer only. Saved ✓

3. First-Day Writing (this one you turn in, and it's required). Take the two writes above and shape a short reflection (a page or so) on your past experiences with writing and how you feel about it now, where you're confident, where you're not, what you hope to get better at. This is how I get to know you as a writer, and it's not graded on polish. Turn it in on AsULearn by Tuesday at 12:00 p.m. This is the one assignment all term that isn't due on a Sunday, and it's how I confirm you're actually in the class.

That's Week 1. You cleared out some myths and put real words on the page, both. Next week we start finding the material for your first essay. Glad you're here.

Writing is discovery

Donald Murray and Peter Elbow taught that you write to find out what you think, the draft is the thinking itself, not a report of thinking you already did. That's why "just start" beats "plan it all first." Your ideas take their real shape on the page, not before it.

Writing is learnable

The "born writer" myth quietly hands people permission to give up. What teachers and researchers keep finding: writing improves with deliberate practice and feedback, the two things this course is built to give you. Where you start is not where you'll finish.

Writing is social and rhetorical

A core idea in the field (Adler-Kassner & Wardle): writing always happens for an audience, in a situation, to do something. Even a diary makes choices about words. So "express yourself" and "reach a reader" work together, good writing does both at once.

Writing to clear your head

Ramirez & Beilock (2011) found students who spent a few minutes writing about their worries before a high-pressure test performed better, because offloading worry onto the page frees up working memory for the task at hand. Getting the mess out of your head and onto paper is a genuine cognitive tool.

Why write about your values?

It's called a values affirmation, and it's one of the better-studied brief writing exercises there is (Cohen, Garcia & Sherman). A few minutes writing about something you genuinely value, near the start of a demanding course, has been shown to improve performance and a sense of belonging, with the biggest benefit for students who worry they don't fit. Short, real, and for you.

Freewriting

Writing fast and without stopping to judge whether it's "good", a technique from Peter Elbow and Ken Macrorie. Keeping your hand moving keeps your inner critic from interrupting, so you find what you actually think and say truer things. It's the engine of invention: you can't shape material you haven't generated yet.
