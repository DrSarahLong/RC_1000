Sentences, Polish & Turning It In

The big re-seeing is done. Now the satisfying part: sharpening the actual sentences so the writing feels as clear as the thinking behind it. Then you format it and hand it in.

This is the final week of your first essay. A read-aloud, a tightening pass, and a format check, then it's finished.

A word before you polish

You did the hard work last week: cutting, reordering, finding what the essay is really about. This week is lighter and more fun. You're not tearing anything down, you're buffing what's already there until it shines. Go at your own pace.

Part 1 · Learn

Edit the sentences, then proofread

Three jobs, biggest to smallest: revise the whole (done), edit the sentences (this week), proofread the typos (last, right before you submit). Doing them in that order means you never polish a sentence you were about to cut. Here's the sentence-level work.

Four moves that sharpen almost any draft:

  • When two or three sentences make the same point, keep the best one and cut the rest, even a line you love.
  • Cut the padding ("due to the fact that" → "because"). Fewer words, more force.
  • Vary the rhythm. If every sentence is the same length, the writing drones. Let some run long, then land one short and hard.
  • Fix the two that make writing look amateur: and .

Two quick models. Read the "before," picture your fix, then check the "after."

Kill your darlings (cut the repetition):

BeforeThe room went silent. The quiet pressed down on all of us. Nobody said a word for a long moment.
AfterThe room went silent. Nobody said a word.

Tighten (same meaning, half the words):

BeforeDue to the fact that it was getting late, I made the decision to finally stop writing for the night.
AfterIt was getting late, so I stopped writing.

Want more? Your Craft Toolkit has before/after models for all of these, including a "kill your darlings" and a "punctuate dialogue" card.

Why tighter sentences actually read better

Readers understand a sentence fastest when it opens with something familiar and puts the new information at the end, what linguists Haviland and Clark (1974) called the . Padding and repetition bury the new part and make the reader work harder than they should. And one low-tech trick catches more errors than any other: Your ears catch what your eyes skip.

How your essay should look on the page

Formatting counts, because how a piece looks changes how it reads. A wall of gray text pushes a reader away before they read a word. Use the course house style on every essay:

  • Single-spaced, 12-point, in a readable serif (Goudy Old Style or Times New Roman), with one-inch margins.
  • Real paragraphs. One line break between them, never a double hard return that leaves a big gap, and never one giant block of text. If a paragraph runs past half a page, it is probably two ideas. Break it.
  • An in the top-left of page one, a centered title, and your last name plus page number in the top-right (in Google Docs: Insert → Page numbers).
  • Name your file like this: LASTNAME_FIRST INITIAL_Project Description, for example Long_S_Revised Essay #1. Use that format on every draft and final version.

Kill your darlings

A famous piece of writing advice (often traced to Arthur Quiller-Couch): cut the lines you're most in love with when they don't serve the piece, especially when two or three sentences repeat the same idea. Keep the strongest one, cut the rest, even the pretty one. Save it in a scrap file if it hurts too much; it might have a home somewhere else.

Concision

Saying it in the fewest words that keep the meaning and the music. Common padding to cut: "due to the fact that" (→ because), "in order to" (→ to), "the reason is because" (→ because), "at this point in time" (→ now). Tightening isn't about being short for its own sake; it's about removing what the reader has to wade through to reach your point.

Comma splice

Joining two complete sentences with only a comma: "It was late, I kept writing." Both halves could stand alone, so a comma is too weak to hold them. Three fixes: a period (late. I kept), a semicolon (late; I kept), or a joining word (late, but I kept). Once you can hear it, you'll catch it everywhere.

Punctuating dialogue

When a tag like "she said" follows the quote, the comma (or ? or !) goes inside the quotation marks: "We should go," she said. Never let the closing quote bump straight into the tag ("We should go" she said). New speaker, new paragraph. These small rules are how a reader can tell you've read a lot of dialogue.

The given-new contract

Haviland & Clark (1974) showed readers process a sentence fastest when it starts with something already familiar (the "given") and puts the new information at the end. Practically: link each sentence to the one before it, and land the fresh idea last. It's why "flow" isn't magic, it's a pattern you can build on purpose.

Read it out loud

The single most effective proofreading move. Your eyes autocorrect what you meant to write; your ears don't. Reading aloud (or letting your device read it to you) surfaces run-ons, missing words, clunky rhythm, and repetition. Every spot you trip over is a spot your reader will trip too. Do this last, right before you submit.

MLA heading & format

A standard way to format an academic paper (Modern Language Association style): a four-line header (your name, instructor, course, date) in the top-left of page one, a centered title, and your last name plus page number in the top-right of every page. One house-style tweak for this class: we single-space (standard MLA is double-spaced), but everything else holds. If your essay quotes sources, MLA also governs the in-text citations and Works Cited, and you'll use that fully in the next project.
