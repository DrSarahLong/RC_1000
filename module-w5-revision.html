RC 1000 · Week 5 · Module

Revision I: Re-Seeing Your Essay

You have a messy first draft of your personal essay. This week you don't fix commas. You step back and see the whole thing again, and decide what it's really about.

Plan on a few sittings this week: read, practice, then revise. Work at your own pace, but don't leave it all for Sunday.

Before you start

Last week you wrote something honest, and re-reading your own words can feel tender. That's normal. Go at your own pace, take breaks, and remember: a rough draft isn't a verdict on you. It's raw material, exactly what it's supposed to be.

Part 1 · Learn

Revision means seeing again, not tidying up

Most people think "revising" means fixing spelling and swapping a few words. That's , and it comes later. is bigger: it's re-seeing the whole essay. Donald Murray, who shaped how writing is taught, put it bluntly: writing is rewriting. Your first draft was how you found out what you had to say. Revision is where you actually say it.

Editing (later)

  • Fix typos & commas
  • Swap a weak word
  • Tighten a sentence
  • Stays on the surface

Revising (this week)

  • What is this really about?
  • What's buried? What should open the essay?
  • Cut, reorder, deepen
  • Changes the whole shape

The researcher studied how real writers revise and found a sharp split: beginners mostly edit words on the surface, while experienced writers re-see and restructure, cutting whole sections, changing the order, discovering what they didn't know they thought. This week you practice the experienced move.

Why this is hard (and the fix)

You can't re-see a draft you're standing inside, you already know what you meant, so you can't tell what's actually on the page. Psychologists call the fix (Kross & Ayduk): create distance from your own writing and you suddenly judge it clearly. Set it aside for a day, read it out loud, read it as if a stranger wrote it. That distance is a skill, and it's the whole game this week.

Two craft moves to watch for while you re-see. Editing can't fix these; only revision can. Both live in your Craft Toolkit if you want more models.

1. The arc: does the essay build, or just list?

A personal essay isn't a timeline ("then, then, then"). Something should shift, and tension should rise before it does. Let one line be shorter and harder than the rest so the turn lands.

Flat listFreshman year I was shy. Then I joined debate. Then I got more confident. Now I speak up in class.
An arcFreshman year I ate lunch facing the wall. Debate didn't fix that; my hands still shook at every tournament. But somewhere around the fourth loss I stopped apologizing before I spoke. I only noticed months later, in a class where I raised my hand before I'd decided to.

2. The ending: is it earned, or a tidy bow?

The most common way an essay goes false is a neat life-lesson at the end. When you feel a bow forming, question it instead. The unfinished, honest thought is stronger than the tidy one.

Tidy bowLooking back, I realize my grandmother's death taught me to cherish every moment with the people I love.
EarnedI'd like to say her death taught me to cherish every moment. Mostly it taught me that I'm already forgetting her voice, and that I keep her last voicemail without playing it, because playing it would use it up.

Part 3 · Practice

Sharpen your eye on someone else's draft first

Revising your own writing is hard because you're too close. So warm up on a stranger's. Open Fix This Draft, a weak first draft of a personal essay, and find what a good reviser would catch. You'll spot the exact problems that are probably hiding in your own draft too.

Open Fix This Draft → Or try the Transfer Drill →

Both work the same way: you catch the problems in a stranger's draft first, where it's easy, then turn that eye on your own.

Earned ending, or tidy bow?

Your reader can feel the difference between an ending that stays honest and one that ties a neat ribbon on the experience. Read each closing line and decide. Commit, then Check.

"In the end, this experience made me a stronger and better person."

Tidy bow. "Stronger and better person" is the ribbon the assignment asks you to resist. What actually changed, and what didn't?

"I still don't know if I forgave him or just got tired of being angry. Some days it feels like the same thing."

Earned. It stays inside the real, unresolved question instead of resolving it too fast. More true, and more interesting.

"And that's how I learned that hard work always pays off."

Tidy bow. It's a slogan, not a discovery. Did the work pay off, or did you also get lucky? Interrogate it.

"I keep the trophy in a drawer now. I can't tell if that's humility or just that I never decided what winning was worth."

Earned. A concrete image (the drawer) plus a real question it won't quite answer. It trusts the reader.

Step 3: make the big moves. Now revise for meaning, not commas:

  1. Cut the deadwood. The warm-up, the summary, the paragraphs that don't earn their place. Be brave.
  2. Open on the heat. Find your strongest moment (often buried near the end) and move it up, or start there.
  3. Deepen it. Give your real subject more room, slow it down, add the specific, shown detail.

Editing vs. revising vs. proofreading

Three different jobs, done big to small. Revising works on ideas and structure (this week). Editing works on sentences, word choice, flow (next week). Proofreading catches typos, last. Doing them in that order saves you from polishing a paragraph you were about to cut.

Re-seeing

"Revision" literally means seeing again. Donald Murray: writing is rewriting. The first draft is how you discover what you have to say; revision is where you shape it into something a reader can follow. It works on the whole, its point, shape, and order, before the commas.

Nancy Sommers on revision

In her classic study (your reading this week), Sommers found beginners treat revision as fixing words on the surface, while experienced writers revise for meaning, cutting, reordering, and discovering new ideas mid-revision. The takeaway that changes everything: revise the whole before you revise the words.

Self-distancing

You can't fairly judge writing you're standing inside, you know what you meant, so you read your intention instead of your words. Kross & Ayduk's research shows that creating distance (time, reading aloud, asking "what is this writer trying to say?") lets you evaluate and improve your own work as if it were someone else's. It's a learnable move, and it's the heart of revision.

Read it out loud

Your eyes skip what your ears catch. Reading a draft aloud (or letting your device read it to you) forces you to hear every word, and it surfaces clunky sentences, missing words, and places where the essay drifts. Every spot you stumble is a spot your reader will too.

Freewriting

Writing fast and without stopping to think about being "good," a technique from Peter Elbow and Ken Macrorie. You keep your hand moving so your editor brain can't interrupt, and you find what you actually think. Here it's a tool for re-seeing: freewrite your reaction to your own draft and you'll say truer things than a careful, self-conscious note would let you.

Reverse outline

An outline you make after drafting, to reveal the structure you actually built. Go paragraph by paragraph and write, in one line, the job each does. Laid out together, the gaps, repetition, and out-of-order pieces jump out, and it's far easier to rearrange a list of one-liners than to rewrite finished prose.

[CLAIM?]: interrogate it

A note you leave in your own margin wherever you've asserted a tidy conclusion ("it made me stronger," "everything happens for a reason"). The bracket isn't the work; it's the flag. The work is writing a few sentences into it, questioning the claim: Is it true? Always? What's the counter-example from my own life? That's where a personal essay does its real thinking.
