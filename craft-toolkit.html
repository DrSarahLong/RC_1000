RC 1000 · Craft Toolkit

How Good Writing Actually Works

The expressive writing gets your story out of your body and onto the page. Craft is what makes it worth another person's time to read. This is your reference shelf for the second part: the specific moves that turn true material into a real essay.

How to use this page. Each move below shows a "before" and an "after" of the same sentence. Don't skip to the fix. Read the before, decide what you'd change, and only then hit Show the fix. You'll learn ten times more from a guess you tested than from an answer you were handed. Come back here whenever you're drafting or revising, these are the notes I'd write in your margins, gathered in one place.

First · What a personal essay actually is

Not a diary, not a lesson

A diary just records what happened. A five-paragraph theme just proves a point. A personal essay does something harder and more interesting: it thinks on the page. Here's what real ones have in common.

You're on the page as a specific person thinking in real time, not a voice narrating from behind glass.
"I want to say I forgave him. I'm not sure the word fits what actually happened."
Self meeting the world. It isn't only about you. It's you making contact with something outside you, a person, a place, an event, and what that contact reveals.
The grandmother, the field, the diagnosis: the outside thing is how you get at the inside one.
Self-discovery. The essay figures something out. You know something at the end you didn't at the start, or you admit you still don't.
"I started this sure I was the calm one. I'm ending it wondering if calm was just fear that learned to sit still."
Show and tell. The image and what you make of it. A scene with no reflection is a video clip; reflection with no scene is a lecture.
It's true, and it reads true. You don't tidy your life into a lesson it didn't actually teach.
The shape follows the thinking. A personal essay can be a letter, a list, a braided story. The form is a choice you get to make.
The suspense isn't "what happens next." It's "what will she understand." A real question pulls the reader all the way through.
"Was it the injury that changed me, or just the first thing I was willing to blame?"

Second · The craft moves

Nine fixes that do the most work

Every one of these came up again and again in real student drafts. Learn them once and you'll catch them in your own writing for the rest of your life.

1

Start in a scene

Cut the opener that announces the essay. Begin where something is actually happening.

BeforeA meaningful experience that shaped who I am was the summer I spent working at my uncle's auto shop.
Where would you start it instead?
AfterThe first car I ever worked on had a wasp nest behind the glovebox. My uncle handed me the wrench anyway. "They're more scared of you," he said. They were not.
Why it worksThe before tells the reader an essay is coming. The after drops them straight into a specific morning with a wrench, a wasp nest, and an uncle who's about to be wrong. Start where the story starts, not one throat-clear before it.
2

Scene over summary

A summary flattens a hundred days into one tired sentence. Pick one real moment and let it carry the whole stretch of time.

BeforeThat whole year, things at home were tense and money was tight.
What single moment could stand in for the whole year?
AfterAt dinner my mother would ask how Dad's day was, and the length of the pause before he answered told me whether we were allowed to laugh that night.
Why it works"Tense" and "tight" are labels for the year. The pause before he answers is the year itself, in one image the reader can feel. One scene beats ten summaries.
3

Show and tell

Showing puts us in your body. Telling says what it meant. You need both: the scene earns the reflection, and the reflection tells us why the scene mattered.

BeforeThe first game of the season felt electric.
Show us "electric," then tell us what it meant to you.
AfterMy knee bounced under the table, and every time the crowd noise spiked in my headphones I leaned in like I could shove the ball forward myself. I had never cared about anything this uselessly, this much.
Why it works"Felt electric" is a label you hand the reader. The bouncing knee and the leaning show it; the last line tells us the meaning (caring uselessly, and being surprised by it). Show first, then land the point.
4

Specific over abstract

Abstractions ("love," "strong," "conditional") are concepts. Readers feel things, not concepts. Trade the concept for the concrete thing it's made of.

BeforeMy dad was always hard to please.
What exact thing did he do that "hard to please" is standing in for?
AfterMy dad clapped for my brother's home runs with his whole body, up off the bleacher. For my strikeouts he'd just nod once, like he'd expected them.
Why it works"Hard to please" tells us your verdict. The standing ovation versus the single nod lets us reach that verdict ourselves, which is far more convincing than being told.
5

Shape an arc, vary the register

"I won, then I won again, then I won again" is a list, not a story. Let tension build, then break it with a shorter, harder line. Same-length, same-tone sentences flatline.

BeforeI kept getting better at chess and winning more and more games and it felt really good to keep improving.
Where's the turn? Where should a short, hard sentence land?
AfterI won my first tournament game by accident. The second on purpose. By the fifth I'd stopped shaking hands like I was surprised. Then a twelve-year-old beat me in nineteen moves and smiled like it was nothing. I wanted to quit. I wanted to play her again. Both, at once.
Why it worksThe wins build (rising rhythm), then one sentence breaks the pattern ("Then a twelve-year-old beat me"). The short closing lines change the pace and hold two feelings at once. Rhythm is meaning.
6

Resist the tidy moral

The neat life-lesson ending is the most common way an essay goes false. When you feel a bow forming, instead. The honest question is more interesting than the tidy answer.

BeforeIn the end, moving to a new state made me stronger, and I learned everything happens for a reason.
Do you actually believe that? What's the truer, messier version?
AfterI want to say the move made me stronger. But maybe I just got used to it, and "stronger" is the word I reach for so it doesn't sound like I lost something I never got back.
Why it worksThe before wraps the experience in a bow and asks the reader to stop thinking. The after stays inside the real question. That's where the essay is still alive, so end nearer to there.
7

Kill your darlings

When two or three sentences do the same job, keep the best one and cut the rest, even the pretty ones. Repetition of thought drains momentum.

BeforeThe distance broke something between us. The miles wore us down until we were strangers. The long drives home alone finally ended what we had.
Which one sentence would you keep? Cut the other two.
AfterThe distance broke something between us. By spring we were strangers who used to be in love.
Why it worksThree sentences circling "the distance ended us" is one idea wearing three coats. Two clean lines land harder. "Kill your darlings" means cut the repetition even when the cut line was good, save it for something it can actually do.
8

Paragraph on the shift

A new paragraph is a signal to the reader: new moment, new idea, breathe here. Start one every time the scene, the time, or the thought changes. A wall of text buries your best moments.

BeforeThe kitchen still smelled like the garlic she'd burned and I hadn't moved from the chair in an hour and then my phone buzzed and it was the hospital saying she was stable and I don't remember standing up but I remember the parking lot, how cold the door handle was, how I'd driven halfway there before I realized I was still in my slippers.
Mark the two places where a new paragraph should start.
AfterThe kitchen still smelled like the garlic she'd burned. I hadn't moved from the chair in an hour.

Then my phone buzzed. It was the hospital, saying she was stable.

I don't remember standing up. I remember the parking lot, how cold the door handle was, how I'd driven halfway there before I realized I was still in my slippers.
Why it worksThree moments (the kitchen, the call, the drive) now get three paragraphs and room to breathe. The reader can feel each one instead of drowning in a single block.
9

Punctuate it like you've read some

Two small mechanics that make writing look amateur when they're wrong: dialogue tags and . Fix them once and they're fixed forever.

Before"You'll understand when you're older" she said.

It was my first time on a plane, I gripped the armrest the whole way.
What punctuation is missing or misused in each?
After"You'll understand when you're older," she said.

It was my first time on a plane. I gripped the armrest the whole way.
Why it worksDialogue: the comma (or ? or !) goes inside the quotes before the tag, never a bare word bumping into "she said." Comma splice: two complete sentences can't be joined with just a comma, use a period, a semicolon, or a joining word (and, but, so).

One last thing. You won't do all nine in a first draft, and you shouldn't try. Get the true thing down first (that's the expressive part). Then come back here with the reviser's eye and work one move at a time. Craft isn't talent you're born with. It's a short list of decisions, and now you have the list.

Personal presence

The sense that a real, particular person is on the page, thinking and feeling in the moment, not a neutral narrator reporting facts. It's the difference between "The event was difficult" and "I keep replaying it, and I still can't decide if I handled it well." Presence is mostly voice plus honesty about what you don't know.

Veracity

Truthfulness, in two senses. Factual: it happened, or close enough that you're not inventing a cleaner story. And emotional: it reads true, you didn't flatten a messy experience into a neat moral it never taught you. Readers can smell a tidied-up life. The messy true version is always more powerful than the polished false one.

Mutability of form

"Mutable" means able to change shape. A personal essay doesn't have one required structure. It can be a letter to someone, a numbered list, a braid of two stories told side by side, or a straight chronological narrative. The shape should follow your thinking. Example: an essay about never being able to reach your father might take the form of an unsent letter, because the form itself says something the content can't.

Intellectual plot

Regular plot is "what happens next." Intellectual plot is "what will the writer come to understand." It's the engine of an essay where not much "happens." The reader keeps going because a real question is unresolved: Did the injury change me, or did I just need something to blame? Plant that question early and circle it, and even a quiet essay has suspense.

Throat-clearing

The warm-up sentences a writer says before the real essay begins, usually an announcement of the topic: "A meaningful experience I had was…," "Ever since I was little…," "This essay is about…." They're the sound of a writer clearing their throat before speaking. Almost always, your real first sentence is two or three lines down, where something actually happens. Find it and start there.

Interrogate the claim

To question your own statement instead of just asserting it. When you write a neat conclusion ("hard work always pays off"), stop and ask it hard questions: Is that true? Always? How do I know? What's the counter-example from my own life? In this course you'll mark those spots with a bracket like [CLAIM?]. The real work is writing a few sentences into that bracket, not stopping at the flag. That's where the thinking gets interesting.

Comma splice

Joining two complete sentences with only a comma: "It was my first time on a plane, I gripped the armrest." Both halves could stand alone, so a comma is too weak to hold them. Three fixes: a period (plane. I gripped), a semicolon (plane; I gripped), or a joining word (plane, and I gripped). Once you can hear it, you'll never unhear it.
