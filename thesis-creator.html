The Thesis Creator

"Thesis" means something different in each essay you'll write this term. This helps you build the right kind for the genre you're in. Pick one to start.

What this does, and doesn't. It won't tell you whether your idea is the right one, no tool can. It teaches you what each kind of thesis needs, shows you real examples, and gives you a frame to build yours. You make the calls. Everything saves only in your browser.

The five-paragraph thesis

The kind you were probably taught in high school: one sentence at the end of your introduction that flatly announces what the essay will prove ("In this essay, I will argue three reasons why…"). It works for a timed exam. But it's only one species of thesis, and it's the wrong one for a personal essay, where the controlling idea is revealed, not announced.

Controlling idea

The single insight that governs a personal essay, the answer to "so what, why does this still matter to me?" You often never state it outright in the essay; you let the scenes reveal it. But you have to KNOW it, or the essay wanders. It's a compass for you, the writer, more than a sentence for the reader.

Debatable

A claim is only a thesis if a reasonable person could disagree with it. "Social media affects teens" isn't debatable (everyone agrees), so it's a topic, not a thesis. "Schools should ban phones in class" is debatable, someone could argue the other side, so it can anchor an argument.

Michel de Montaigne

The French writer (1533–1592) who invented the personal essay. He retreated to his tower library and wrote hundreds of pieces trying to work out what he actually thought, about friendship, fear, his own habits, everything. He called them Essais. The exploring, first-person, changing-its-mind-on-the-page voice you use in a personal essay comes straight from him.

Where "essay" comes from

From the French essayer: to try, to attempt, to test. Montaigne meant it literally. An essay is an attempt to work something out, not a finished proof. That is why a personal essay can wander, question itself, and change its mind. You are trying an idea on, wrestling with it, rather than defending a verdict.
