RC 1000 · Week 8 · Module

Fair Argument & Fallacies

A lighter week over the break. You have a claim; now let's make sure it's fair. We'll name the sneaky moves that make an argument collapse under pressure, and think about where honest persuasion ends and manipulation begins.

Fall Break week: one reading, a quick clinic, a short audit of your own argument. Breathe, then dig in.

Before you start

This week you'll turn a critical eye on your own argument, which can sting a little when you're attached to it. That's normal and useful. Finding a weak spot now is a gift to your future draft, not a verdict on your thinking.

Part 1 · Learn

Fallacies: the moves that look convincing and aren't

A is a move in an argument that feels persuasive but doesn't hold up, a shortcut that skips the actual reasoning. Spotting them protects you two ways: you can answer them when others use them, and you can catch them in your own drafts before a reader does. Here are the six that show up most:

A big conclusion from too little. "My phone distracted me, so phones ruin everyone's focus."
Twisting the other side into something easy to knock down. "People who want later start times just want lazy teenagers."
Attacking the person, not the argument. "You can't trust her housing plan, she's only a sophomore."
Only two options when there are more. "Either we ban phones or nobody learns anything."
One step must lead to disaster. "Let students retake one test and soon grades mean nothing."
Feeling standing in for evidence. "Think of the children", with no actual argument attached.

Where persuasion turns into manipulation

Here's the tension in our kind of writing: we use feeling on purpose. Scene, voice, a story that lands in the body, those are persuasive by design. That's allowed. In classical rhetoric, emotional appeal () is one of the three legitimate ways to persuade, alongside logic and credibility. The catch: pathos is meant to work with your reasons, not replace them.

So the fair line is this: emotion is honest when it's true and points at a real claim you can defend. It becomes when it distorts the facts, exploits the reader, or does the job your evidence should be doing. And the fallacy that tempts personal-argument writers most is hasty generalization: your one real experience is evidence, but it isn't proof about everyone. Claim what your story can carry, and no more.

Unfair (straw man + false dilemma)Anyone against later start times just doesn't care about kids. Either we start school at nine or we admit we've given up on teenagers.
Fair (answers the real objection)People worry later start times will scramble bus routes and after-school jobs, and those are real costs. I still think the trade is worth it, and here's the morning that convinced me.

Part 3 · Practice

The fallacy clinic

Name the move. For each statement, pick the fallacy it commits. Commit, then Check, the feedback explains why.

"My roommate never cleans, so clearly nobody our age has any respect for shared space."

Hasty generalization. One messy roommate can't support a claim about an entire generation. The sample is way too small.

"People who support later school start times just want an excuse for teenagers to be lazy."

Straw man. It swaps the real argument (sleep science, attention) for a dumb version ("they're lazy") that's easy to mock.

"Either we ban phones from campus completely, or we accept that no one will ever learn anything again."

False dilemma. It offers two extremes as if nothing sits between them. Plenty does, phone-free class time, for one.

"You don't have to take her argument about dorm rules seriously, she's only a freshman."

Ad hominem. It attacks who she is instead of what she said. A freshman's argument can still be correct.

"If the professor lets one student turn in a paper late, soon the whole class will stop meeting deadlines and the course will fall apart."

Slippery slope. It treats one small allowance as an unstoppable slide to disaster, with no reason the steps must follow.

Audit your own argument

Fallacy

A flaw in reasoning that makes an argument invalid even when it sounds convincing. Fallacies persuade by feel, not by logic, which is why they're worth learning to spot. Example: "Everyone's doing the reading, so it must be worth doing" sounds reasonable but is a fallacy (popularity isn't proof). Naming the move is how you defuse it.

Hasty generalization

Drawing a broad conclusion from a sample too small to support it, the classic risk when you argue from personal experience. Your one story is real evidence, but it can't prove a claim about everyone. Example: "My online class was a disaster, so online learning doesn't work." The fix: qualify ("for students like me," "in my experience") or gather more cases.

Straw man

Replacing your opponent's real argument with a weaker, distorted version that's easier to attack, then knocking down the fake one. Example: someone argues for later start times to help sleep, and you "answer" them with "so you think teenagers should just sleep all day?" The fix: argue against the strongest, fairest version of the other side.

Ad hominem

Latin for "to the person." Attacking the arguer instead of the argument. Example: "Why would we listen to her about parking? She doesn't even own a car." Whether she owns a car has nothing to do with whether her reasoning is sound. Address the claim, not the character.

False dilemma

Presenting only two options (usually two extremes) when more exist, forcing a choice that isn't real. Example: "Either you support the new meal plan or you want students to go hungry." There are obviously other positions. The fix: name the middle ground the dilemma is hiding.

Slippery slope

Claiming one small step will inevitably trigger a chain of worse ones, without showing why each step must follow. Example: "If we allow one club to meet after hours, soon the building will never close." Sometimes a first step really does lead somewhere, but then you have to show why each step follows, rather than assert the slide.

Appeal to emotion (misused)

Using feeling to substitute for a reason, rather than to support one. Emotion itself isn't the problem, our whole approach uses it. It's a fallacy only when the feeling replaces evidence. Example: a flag-waving, tear-jerking appeal that never actually says why the policy is good. Fair version: the feeling makes you care, and a real reason makes the case.

Ethos, pathos, logos

Aristotle's three modes of persuasion: ethos (your credibility), pathos (emotion), and logos (logic and evidence). All three are legitimate, good arguments use all three. Pathos isn't cheating; a story that makes a reader feel the stakes is doing real persuasive work. It just has to travel with logos, not instead of it. Example: the scene shows you the cost; the reasons prove the point.

Persuasion vs. manipulation

Persuasion invites a reader to a conclusion using true feeling and sound reasons; manipulation pushes them there by distorting facts, exploiting emotion, or hiding the weakness of the case. Example: telling a true, moving story to help a reader understand a real problem is persuasion; inventing or exaggerating that story to get the same reaction is manipulation. Same tools, different honesty.
