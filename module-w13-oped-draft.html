RC 1000 · Week 13 · Module

Draft & Revise the Op-Ed

You have a pitch and a skeleton. Now write the thing, about 700 tight words, then cut and sharpen until every line earns its place. This is your last writing project. One project still follows it: the digital portfolio.

Draft this week, revise, and submit Sunday. Short piece, high polish.

Before you start

A short piece feels easy until you try to make every word count. That pressure is the whole point of the op-ed. Draft loosely first, then tighten; nobody writes 700 sharp words on the first pass. You've done the thinking already, this week is craft.

Part 1 · Learn

Short is hard. Punch is harder.

An op-ed runs about 600 to 800 words, which is not much room. So the two skills that matter most this week are (say it in the fewest words that keep the meaning) and (sound like a sharp person talking, not a report). Here's a draft sentence and its rescue:

Flabby (and faceless)It is important to note that, in my own personal opinion, there are a number of different reasons why the city council should perhaps consider the possibility of adding more bus routes.
Tight (and human)The city needs more buses. I've missed three job interviews waiting for one that never came.

Twenty-nine words become fourteen, the hedging vanishes, and a real person appears. Op-ed readers reward that trade every time. When in doubt, cut the wind-up and lead with the concrete.

A short checklist of op-ed moves (you'll do these in the Revision X-Ray below, this is just the what):

  • Cut the throat-clearing. "It is important to note that," "in today's society," "I personally believe", delete on sight.
  • Vary the rhythm. After a couple of longer sentences, drop a short one. It lands.
  • Strong verbs, few adverbs. "Slashed," not "reduced by a lot."
  • Link, don't cite. Your evidence gets a , not an MLA parenthesis. No Works Cited page on an op-ed.
  • Land the . Your last line is the one they'll remember. Make it snap, ideally circling back to your opening image.

Part 3 · Practice

Pick the op-ed sentence

Each pair says the same thing. Choose the one a busy public reader would keep reading. Commit, then Check.

Tighten it:

B. Sixteen words down to eight, same meaning, more force. "Due to the fact that" is always cuttable.

Cut the hedging:

B. An op-ed takes a position. "Kind of," "maybe," "possibly," "a bit", each one shrinks your authority. Say it plainly.

Trade abstract for concrete:

B. "Significant benefits to numerous individuals" is fog. Forty families, last winter, fed, that a reader can see and feel.

Land the ending:

B. A is a flat summary. B is a kicker: short, pointed, and it leaves the argument ringing. End on the snap.

Part 4 · Do

Draft, tighten, submit

  1. Draft the whole thing in your Google Doc, following your Week 12 outline: hook, nut graf, two or three quick points with linked evidence, a one-line counter, a kicker. Don't polish yet, just fill the skeleton.
  2. Check the length below, then trim toward 600 to 800 words. If you're over, that's normal; cutting is where op-eds get good.
  3. Run the Revision X-Ray: highlight your claim, evidence, and counter; confirm every borrowed fact is linked (not left uncited); and hear it read back, does it sound like a person? Does the kicker land?
  4. Polish the headline and the last line. These do the most work per word in the whole piece.

Concision

Saying it in the fewest words that keep the meaning and the music, not being short for its own sake, but cutting what the reader has to wade through. Example: "at this point in time" becomes "now"; "she made the decision to leave" becomes "she left." In a 700-word op-ed, every cut word buys room for one that matters.

Public voice

Writing that sounds like a sharp, warm person talking to you, not an institution issuing a statement. Contractions are fine. Short sentences are welcome. Personality is the point. Example, institutional: "It is recommended that residents utilize public transit." Public voice: "Take the bus. I do, and here's what it taught me." Same content, a human behind it.

Hyperlinked citation (recap)

In public writing you cite by linking, not by MLA. When you state a fact from a source, make the key words a link to it. Example: "arrests dropped by a third after the program opened," where the underlined words link to the report. Clean prose, and a curious reader can check you in one click. No parentheses, no Works Cited page.

Kicker (recap)

The closing line, the snap the reader carries out the door. Short, pointed, and often it echoes your opening image. Example, an op-ed that opened on a late school bus ends: "So cut it from the scoreboard, not the ladder." A weak op-ed trails off with "in conclusion"; a strong one lands its last sentence like a door closing.
