RC 1000 · Week 10 · Module

Writing Yourself Into the Conversation

You have your claim, your experience, and a few solid sources. Now you weave them together, bringing sources into your prose without burying your voice or dropping quotes like bricks. Then you cite them in MLA and draft the whole argument.

The biggest writing week of this project. You'll produce a full rough draft, with sources.

A reminder

Working with sources for the first time can feel stiff, like the essay stops being yours. It doesn't have to. The goal this week is to keep you in charge and let the sources back you up. Your voice still leads.

Part 1 · Learn

You're joining a conversation

Think of your topic as a conversation that's been going on before you walked in. Other people, researchers, writers, experts, have already said things about it. means entering that conversation: you say what they say, then you say what you think, and you show how the two connect. The scholars Gerald Graff and Cathy Birkenstein call this the move, and it's the engine of academic writing. You're not reporting sources like a book report. You're using them to build your case.

Three ways to bring in a source

  • when the exact words matter, they're vivid, precise, or you want to analyze them. Use quotes sparingly.
  • (put one idea fully in your own words) when the point matters more than the phrasing. This is your workhorse.
  • Summarize when you need the gist of a whole study or article in a sentence or two.

All three need a citation, even the paraphrase and the summary. Borrowing an idea counts, the same as borrowing words.

The quote sandwich: never drop a quote naked

The most common source mistake is the : a quotation dropped into a paragraph with nothing around it, left for the reader to interpret. Fix it with a , three layers:

Quote bomb (no framing)Teenagers need more sleep. "Adolescents who start school later score measurably higher on tests of attention" (Wahlstrom 12). My district should start later.
A quote sandwichMy groggy first periods weren't just my problem. As sleep researcher Kyla Wahlstrom found, "adolescents who start school later score measurably higher on tests of attention" (12). In other words, the fog I felt at 7 a.m. shows up in the data as lost learning, which is exactly why my district should push the bell back.

Top slice: your (who's speaking, why they matter). Filling: the quote or paraphrase, cited. Bottom slice: your explanation tying it to your claim. The source is the filling; your voice is the bread on both sides.

Citing in MLA, briefly

Every borrowed idea gets an : the author's last name and a page number in parentheses, (Wahlstrom 12), right where the borrowing ends. If your lead-in already names the author ("As Wahlstrom found…"), the parentheses just need the page: (12). Each in-text citation points to a full entry on your page. You don't have to memorize the formatting, that's what the builder below is for.

Part 3 · Practice (A)

Paraphrase, or patchwriting?

A real paraphrase puts the idea in your words and structure. Swapping a few synonyms while keeping the original's shape is , and it counts as plagiarism even with a citation. Here's the original; decide whether each student version is a safe paraphrase or too close.

Original source: "Adolescents' internal clocks shift later during puberty, making early school start times biologically mismatched with teenage sleep."

"Teens' internal clocks shift later during puberty, making early start times biologically mismatched with teenage sleep."

Too close. It just swaps a couple of words and keeps the original sentence's exact structure. A citation won't save it; either quote it directly or truly rewrite it.

"Because puberty pushes a teenager's body clock later, a 7 a.m. first bell fights their biology instead of working with it (Author 4)."

Safe paraphrase. New words, new structure, same idea, and it's cited. This is what a real paraphrase looks like.

"During puberty, adolescents' internal clocks shift to a later time, so early school starts are biologically mismatched with how teenagers sleep."

Too close. Rearranging the clauses and changing "making" to "so" still tracks the original almost word for word. Read it, look away, and write the idea from memory instead.

Synthesis

Combining sources and your own thinking into one flowing argument, rather than listing what each source says one after another. Example of NOT synthesizing: "Source A says X. Source B says Y. Source C says Z." Synthesis: "The research agrees that X, and my own experience shows why that matters here." You're the one connecting the dots, in service of your claim.

They say / I say

Gerald Graff and Cathy Birkenstein's frame for academic writing: start by laying out what others say ("they say"), then respond with your own view ("I say"). Example: "Many people assume phones are harmless in class (they say). My own semester with one on my desk taught me otherwise (I say)." It keeps your writing in conversation instead of talking into a void.

Quoting

Using a source's exact words, inside quotation marks, with a citation. Reserve it for when the wording itself matters, it's striking, precise, or you want to examine it. Example: quote a senator's exact phrasing if you're going to analyze that phrasing. Don't quote a plain fact you could just state; paraphrase that instead. Over-quoting makes an essay read like a scrapbook.

Paraphrasing

Restating one specific idea from a source fully in your own words and sentence structure, then citing it. It's the main way you'll use sources. Example, original: "Funding cuts reduced clinic hours." Paraphrase: "When the budget shrank, the clinic could stay open fewer hours a week (Author 8)." Same idea, genuinely your words. Changing only a few words is patchwriting, not paraphrase.

The quote bomb

Dropping a quotation into your paragraph with no lead-in and no follow-up, leaving the reader to figure out why it's there. Example: My town needs more buses. "Ridership rose 40% after the pilot" (Lee 3). Also parking is hard. The quote just sits there, orphaned. The fix is the quote sandwich: frame it before, explain it after.

The quote sandwich

A three-part way to integrate any source so it never sits naked: (1) a lead-in that introduces it, (2) the quote or paraphrase, cited, (3) your explanation connecting it to your point. Example: "As Lee reports (top), 'ridership rose 40% after the pilot' (3) (filling). That jump is exactly the demand my neighborhood keeps being told doesn't exist (bottom)." Source in the middle, your voice on both sides.

Signal phrase (lead-in)

The words that introduce a source and hand the reader a reason to trust it: "As Dr. Wahlstrom found," "According to a 2014 policy statement," "The sociologist Tressie McMillan Cottom argues." Example: instead of a bare quote, "According to sleep researcher Kyla Wahlstrom, ___." It tells the reader who's talking and why they count, before the borrowed words arrive.

In-text citation

The short parenthetical that marks borrowed material right where it appears: normally the author's last name and a page, (Wahlstrom 12). If you already named the author in your lead-in, just the page: (12). No page (like a web source)? Just the author: (Wahlstrom). Each one matches a full entry on your Works Cited page, so a reader can find the exact source.

Works Cited

The alphabetized list of full source details at the end of an MLA paper, one entry per source you cited. Example entry: Wahlstrom, Kyla. "School Start Time and Sleepy Teens." Archives of Pediatrics, vol. 164, no. 7, 2010, pp. 676-681. Build these as you go with the citation builder; assembling them all at the end is a recipe for a bad night.

Patchwriting

Paraphrasing so lightly that you keep the source's structure and just swap in synonyms. It's a form of plagiarism even when cited, because the sentence is still basically the author's. Example, original: "The policy reduced emissions sharply." Patchwriting: "The rule cut emissions dramatically." Real fix: read it, look away, and write the idea in your own sentence from memory, then check you didn't drift back.

Shitty first draft (recap)

Anne Lamott's permission slip from Project 1: the first draft's only job is to exist. Even now, with sources to juggle, don't try to draft and perfect at once. Get the whole argument down, rough citations and all, then fix it next week. A messy complete draft beats a perfect half.
