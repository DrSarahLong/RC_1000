Finding the Heat

You have pages of raw material. Now the real move: find the one thing this essay is actually about. Not the topic, the point, the thing you discovered while writing.

A shorter week. One good sitting to reread, find your focus, and name it.

Before you start

Go back to last week's freewrites when you're ready and steady. You'll be rereading your own honest material, take it gently, and remember you decide what to carry forward and what to leave.

Part 1 · Learn

A topic isn't a point

"My summer at my grandma's" is a , a subject area. It isn't an essay yet, because it doesn't say anything. What turns a topic into an essay is a : the one thing you came to understand, feel, or reckon with. "My grandma's quiet routines taught me that love is mostly small, repeated acts", now there's an essay.

How you find it: the felt sense

You usually can't decide your point by logic. You find it by feel. The composition researcher Sondra Perl called this the : that bodily "that's it, that's the part that matters" you get when you hit the real thing. In your material, it shows up as heat, the line that surprised you, embarrassed you, or won't let go. Your job this week is to find the heat and name what it means.

The sentence that names it is your . "Working" means it can change, it's a compass, not a contract. But having one keeps your draft from wandering.

One thing about the word "thesis." You were probably taught the five-paragraph kind: a stiff sentence that announces "I will argue three reasons." A personal-essay thesis is a quieter animal. The word comes from the French essayer, to try, to attempt, to wrestle with an idea. , who invented the form in 1580, wasn't proving a fixed point; he was thinking on the page. Your thesis is a "so what?" anchor that ties your specific story to a larger, human truth. It is:

  • Reflective, it shows how an experience changed your perspective or revealed a value.
  • Subtle and fluid, it often blends into the narrative instead of standing out as a stiff declaration.
  • Built on tension, a realization, a complication, or a shift in how you see the world.

Three moves it can make (each ties a small story to a bigger truth):

  • The realization shift. "I used to think safety meant staying silent, but learning to fix motorcycles taught me that true security comes from making some noise."
  • The micro-to-macro insight. "Cleaning up my grandmother's crowded kitchen after she passed didn't just clear out old spices; it forced me to stop holding onto versions of myself I had outgrown."
  • The question-driven anchor. "Trying to teach my stubborn dog to sit didn't show me how to control an animal; it revealed my own deep fear of losing control."

Where does the thesis go? In an argument, it's almost always up front, stated by the end of the introduction. A personal essay is looser: your controlling idea might open the essay, surface halfway through as a realization, or arrive only at the end, after the scenes have earned it. Sometimes you never state it outright at all. What matters is that you know it from the first draft, so every scene points somewhere.

Part 3 · Practice

Topic, or controlling idea?

This is subtler than it looks, plenty of things that sound like a point are still just a topic in disguise. For each, decide: is it a topic (a subject, or a vague claim that says nothing only you could say), or a real controlling idea (specific, with a point or tension a reader can follow)? Commit, then Check.

"My grandmother was one of the most important people in my life."

Topic. It sounds like a claim, but "one of the most important people" is generic, almost anyone could write it about someone. It states importance without saying the specific thing only you could say.

"Taking care of my grandmother taught me a lot about patience and family."

Topic in disguise, the classic trap. It has thesis shape ("taught me a lot about…"), but "a lot about patience and family" is vague. What, exactly, did it teach you that you didn't already believe?

"Caring for my grandmother as she forgot my name turned me into the adult in our relationship, and part of me resented her for it."

A controlling idea. It's specific (she forgot your name; you became "the adult") and it carries real tension, the resentment you're not proud of. That honest complication is what a reader will follow.

"Everyone called my grandmother difficult, but her stubbornness was the exact thing that carried our family through its worst year."

A controlling idea. The "everyone said X, but actually Y" turn takes a real position, it reframes something specific (her stubbornness) into a claim a reader could even push back on.

Topic vs. essay

A topic is a subject area ("my grandmother," "soccer," "moving"). It's where you start, not where you land. A topic becomes an essay only when you find something to say about it, a point, a discovery, a tension. Don't confuse having a topic with having an essay.

Controlling idea

The single main point that governs the whole essay, the thing everything else serves. In a personal essay it's usually something you came to understand, not a thesis you argue in five paragraphs. If a paragraph doesn't connect to your controlling idea, that's a sign it (or the idea) needs to change.

Felt sense

Sondra Perl's term for the bodily, not-yet-worded sense of what you're trying to say, the "that's it" you feel before you can explain it. Good writers learn to trust it: when a line gives you a small jolt, that's the felt sense telling you you've hit something real. Follow the jolt.

Working thesis

A one-sentence statement of what your essay is about, written early and held loosely. "Working" is the key word: it guides your draft but is expected to change as you discover more. Having one keeps a personal essay from wandering; being willing to revise it keeps the essay honest.

Where "essay" comes from

From the French essayer: to try, to attempt, to test. Montaigne meant it literally. An essay is an attempt to work something out, not a finished proof. That is why a personal essay can wander, question itself, and change its mind. You are trying an idea on, wrestling with it, rather than defending a verdict.

Michel de Montaigne

The French writer (1533–1592) who invented the personal essay. He retreated to his tower library and wrote hundreds of pieces trying to work out what he actually thought, about friendship, fear, his own habits, everything. He called them Essais. The exploring, first-person, changing-its-mind-on-the-page voice you use in a personal essay comes straight from him.
