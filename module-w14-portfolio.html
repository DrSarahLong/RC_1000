RC 1000 · Week 14 · Module

Build Your Portfolio

Your writing has lived in private Google Docs all semester. Now it gets a home: a real, shareable website you build yourself, for free, with no code. This week you set it up, write two new pages, and move your work in.

First of three portfolio weeks. This week: build the site and load your essays.

You can do this

If "build a website" makes your stomach drop, breathe. Google Sites is drag-and-drop, closer to making a slide deck than to coding. If you can type and click, you can build this. And the payoff is real: a link you can put on a resume, a job application, or a grad-school form.

Part 1 · Learn

What a portfolio is, and why it's worth it

A is a small website that collects your best work and presents you as a writer. Yours will hold your three revised pieces plus a short reflection on how you grew. It does two jobs: it shows your range (a personal essay, an argument, an op-ed), and it gives you one link to hand anyone who asks "can you write?"

Design serves reading

A portfolio is : words, layout, images, and color make meaning together. You don't need to be a designer. You need a few habits that keep the focus on your writing:

  • Use headings and spacing so a reader can scan. Big title, clear section headings, readable body text.
  • Restraint. One theme, one or two fonts, a small color palette. Busy looks amateur; clean looks confident.
  • Dark text on a light background (or the reverse). Never light gray on white.
  • Empty space isn't wasted; it's what makes a page feel calm and readable. Don't cram.
  • Any image gets so screen-reader users (and search engines) know what it shows.
Why design does real work

Stanford's Web Credibility research (Fogg et al., 2002) asked thousands of people how they judge whether a website is trustworthy. The single most-cited factor was visual design, ahead of the actual information. Fair or not, a clean, careful-looking site makes a reader trust the writing inside it. Your design is part of your argument for yourself.

Part 3 · Practice

Good design or bad design?

Before you build, tune your eye. For each choice, decide: does it help a reader, or hurt them? Commit, then Check.

Using five different fonts and three bright background colors so the site "pops."

Hurts. Visual noise makes a reader work harder and trust you less. One or two fonts and a small palette read as confident, not boring.

Picking one clean theme and using headings to break each page into clear sections.

Helps. Consistency plus a clear hierarchy lets a reader scan and find what they want. This is most of good web design.

Dark gray text on a black background because it looks sleek and moody.

Hurts. Low contrast is hard for everyone to read and impossible for some. Legibility beats mood every time; keep text and background far apart in brightness.

Writing a short alt-text description for the photo on your home page.

Helps. Alt text makes your site usable for people with screen readers, and it's a mark of a thoughtful builder. It takes ten seconds and it's the right thing to do.

Part 4 · Compose your two written pages

Your Bio and your Philosophy of Writing

Two pages hold writing you compose fresh, not your essays. Draft them here first (private, on your computer), then move them onto your site.

1. Your Bio

A short paragraph introducing you as a writer and a person: where you're from, what you care about, what you find yourself writing toward. Warm and specific reads better than formal and vague. A few sentences is plenty.

2. Your Philosophy of Writing

This is the heart of your portfolio: a short piece (a few paragraphs) on what you believe writing is and what it can do, especially its power to reach across difference, tell the truth, and change something in the world. You've spent a semester watching writing heal, argue, and reach a public. Now say what you make of it.

Voices to think with. For as long as people have written, they've argued that writing is a force for good, for justice, for our shared humanity. Read these, then answer them with your own view.

  • James Baldwin (novelist and civil-rights essayist): "You write in order to change the world… If you alter, even by a millimeter, the way people look at reality, then you can change it."
  • Toni Morrison (Nobel-winning novelist): "This is precisely the time when artists go to work… We speak, we write, we do language. That is how civilizations heal."
  • Frederick Douglass (abolitionist who taught himself to read while enslaved): learning to read and write was, he wrote, "the pathway from slavery to freedom."
  • Audre Lorde (poet and activist): "Your silence will not protect you." Language, she argued, is how the unheard claim their power.
  • Maya Angelou (poet and memoirist): "There is no greater agony than bearing an untold story inside you."
  • Anne Frank (diarist, writing in hiding): "I can shake off everything as I write; my sorrows disappear, my courage is reborn."

Use these prompts to find your own position. You don't have to answer all of them, follow the ones with heat:

  • Finish the sentence: "Writing is…" What is it for?
  • Name one time writing changed something, for you, or for the world.
  • Which voice above do you agree with, or argue against, and why?
  • What can writing do about the things that are wrong, injustice, silence, being unseen or misunderstood?
  • End on your own one-line credo: what you believe writing can do.

Part 5 · Build & publish

Build your portfolio, step by step

Work through these on Google Sites (free, and your App State Google account already has it). Tick each as you finish, this saves on your computer.

1.Create the site.
Go to sites.google.com, sign in, and click the blank "+" to start a new site. Give it a name in the top-left, and type a title into the banner (for example, "The Writing of [Your Name]").
2.Add your pages.
In the Pages panel on the right, add a page for each part of your portfolio: Home, Bio, Personal Essay, Argument, Op-Ed, Philosophy of Writing, and Reflection (you'll write the reflection next week). The top navigation menu builds itself from your pages.
3.Move your writing in.
On each essay page, use the Insert panel. Easiest options: paste the text and format it with the Heading and Normal-text styles, or use Insert → From Drive to embed the Google Doc, or upload a PDF. Paste your Bio and Philosophy of Writing drafts (from Part 4) onto their pages too. Keep the formatting simple and consistent.
4.Design it.
Open the Themes panel, pick one clean theme, choose a font style, and set a simple color. Use Heading styles for structure and leave generous whitespace. If you add an image, give it (right-click the image → Add alt text).
5.Write a one-line Home intro.
On the Home page, add a sentence or two: who you are and what this site is. The full reflective introduction comes next week; this is just a placeholder greeting.
6.Publish and get your link.
Click (top-right), choose a web address, and set who can view it to "Anyone with the link." Copy that link, that's your portfolio, live on the internet.
7.Triple-check the link works, for other people.
This is the step everyone skips and regrets. Copy your published link and open it in a private / incognito window (or text it to a friend and ask if it opens). If it demands a login or says "access denied," your site isn't really public yet, fix the sharing to "Anyone with the link," re-publish, and check again. Save that working link; you'll submit the finished portfolio with it at the end of term. A link I can't open is a portfolio I can't grade, so get it working now while it's fresh.

Digital portfolio

A small website that gathers your best work in one place to show what you can do. Writers, designers, and students all keep them. Example: a link on your resume that opens to your three polished essays and a short intro, instead of a professor having to take your word for it. It turns "I can write" into "here, see for yourself."

Multimodal

Communicating through more than one "mode" at once, words plus images, layout, color, and sometimes sound or video. A printed essay is one mode (text); a web page is multimodal. Example: on your op-ed page, the headline's size, a relevant photo, and the spacing all shape how a reader takes in your words. Design becomes part of the meaning.

Visual hierarchy

Arranging a page so the eye knows what's most important and in what order, usually through size and spacing. Example: a big page title, medium section headings, and normal body text tell a reader "start here, then here." Without hierarchy, everything looks equally important, which means nothing stands out. Headings are your main tool.

Contrast (legibility)

The difference in brightness between text and its background. High contrast (dark text on light, or light on dark) is easy to read; low contrast (gray on white, dark gray on black) strains the eyes and fails for readers with low vision. Example: black text on white passes; light-gray text on a white background does not. When unsure, make the difference bigger.

Whitespace

The empty space around and between elements on a page. It isn't wasted room; it's what lets content breathe and reads as calm and professional. Example: a page with wide margins and space between sections feels easy; the same text crammed edge to edge feels stressful. When a page looks busy, the fix is usually more space, not more stuff.

Alt text

A short written description of an image, read aloud by screen readers and shown if the image fails to load. Example: for a photo of your grandmother's kitchen, the alt text might be "a small kitchen with mismatched mugs on open shelves." It makes your site usable for people who can't see the image, and it takes seconds. In Google Sites: right-click the image, choose Add alt text.

Publishing (Google Sites)

Making your site live on the internet at a real web address. In Google Sites, "Publish" is a separate step from editing, your edits don't go public until you click it. Example: you build and rearrange privately, then Publish when it's ready, and choose "Anyone with the link" so the people you share it with can view it. Re-click Publish each time you want new changes to go live.
