Fix This Draft

Revising your own writing is hard because you're too close to it. So first, sharpen your eye on someone else's. Below is a real-ish first draft of a personal essay, "My Trip."

You diagnose it. Drag each label from the bank onto the paragraph you think it describes (on a phone, tap a label, then tap a paragraph's slot). Place all five, then hit Check. The goal isn't fixing commas, it's : what's it about, what's buried, what should go.

Paragraph 4

One morning we went fishing at the lake right as the sun was coming up and the water was really still and my grandma started telling me a story about my mom from when she was a little girl that I had never heard before about a time she ran away from home and it honestly made me see my whole family in a different way.

The real story, buried in a run-on Here is the , the mom story, the fishing morning, but it's trapped in one breathless and stuck at the bottom.
Fix: Open here. Slow it down, give it room, and build the whole essay toward it.

Paragraph 5

In conclusion, my summer at my grandma's was a very memorable experience that I will never forget and it taught me the importance of family.

drop a label here
Formulaic, clichéd ending A canned "In conclusion" plus clichés ("memorable experience I'll never forget," "the importance of family") that restate instead of landing.
Fix: End on a specific image only your fishing-morning story could earn.

The big move isn't fixing these sentences one at a time. It's re-seeing the whole draft. Cut the drive and the day's schedule, open on the lake at sunrise, and make the buried story about your mom the heart. That's what the researcher found separates experienced writers from beginners: beginners swap words, experienced writers re-see and restructure. Now take that same eye to your own draft.

Re-seeing (what revision means)

"Revision" literally means seeing again. It's not proofreading. Donald Murray said writing is rewriting: a first draft is how you find out what you have to say, and revision is where you actually say it. Re-seeing means looking at the whole, its point, shape, and order, before you touch a comma.

Nancy Sommers on revision

In her classic study "Revision Strategies of Student Writers and Experienced Adult Writers" (your reading this week), Sommers found beginners tidy up words on the surface, while experienced writers revise for meaning, cutting, reordering, discovering what they didn't know they thought. The lesson: revise the whole before the words.

Throat-clearing

The warm-up sentences at the start of a draft before the writer gets to the real thing, "This essay is about…," "Ever since I was little…." They announce a topic instead of starting it. The first strong sentence is usually the second or third one; cut everything before it.

Open in a scene

A scene is a specific moment shown in real time, a place, a person, an action, so the reader is there. Compare "This essay is about my grandma" with "The lake was still and gray when my grandma cut the engine." Strong personal essays drop you into a moment instead of introducing the topic.

Clichés

Phrases so worn they've stopped meaning anything: "changed me as a person," "memorable experience I'll never forget," "the importance of family." They feel like writing but say nothing specific. Replace them with the actual concrete thing that happened to you.

Show, don't (only) tell

"Telling" states a conclusion ("my grandma was nice"). "Showing" gives the evidence and lets the reader conclude it ("she saved the crossword for us to do together"). A personal essay lives on shown, specific moments. When you catch a vague summary, ask: what's the scene underneath it?

Comma splice

Two complete sentences joined by only a comma: "we did fun things together, it was a great time." Fix three ways: a period, a semicolon, or a joining word ("…together, and it was…"). We drill sentence fixes like this next week; this week stays on the big picture.

The buried lead

Often the real essay is hiding near the end, in the one paragraph that suddenly has heat. Writers "warm up" for a page before they reach what they came to say. The move: find that buried moment and rebuild the essay around it, up front. It's the most common, most powerful personal-essay fix.

Run-on sentence

A sentence that keeps adding "and… and… and…" until it can't breathe, like paragraph 4's single 60-word sentence. Fine in a first draft (you rushing to get it down). In revision you break it up and let the important beats land.
