Finding Your Material

Your first essay is a personal one. But you don't start with a topic, you start by generating honest, specific material and seeing what has life in it. This week you write to find out what you have to say.

Mostly writing this week. Give yourself two or three unhurried sittings.

You're in charge here

This week you'll write about your own life. You choose what to explore and how far to go, nothing you write below is graded or seen by anyone unless you decide to share it. Write toward what has energy for you, and if something feels like too much right now, pick something else. There's no wrong choice.

Part 1 · Learn

Invention: generating before you know the shape

Every essay starts as a pile of raw material, long before it has a point or an order. Writers call the messy generating stage , and the oldest, most reliable way to do it is : write fast, don't stop, don't judge. Peter Elbow and Ken Macrorie showed that keeping your hand moving shuts off your inner critic long enough for the honest, surprising stuff to come out.

Two moves make freewriting even more productive:

  • Freewrite, find the one line with the most , then freewrite again starting from that line. Repeat. Each loop digs closer to what you actually mean.
  • When a memory has a charge, name the emotion in a word or two. Putting a feeling into words (researchers call it affect labeling) makes it clearer and easier to write about, not more overwhelming.
Why "just get it down" works

Your goal this week is quantity and honesty, not quality. That's not laziness, it's how the brain works: trying to generate and judge at the same time jams both. Get the mess onto the page first (this also clears mental space, the same effect that helped students who think better under pressure). You'll shape it later. Right now, dig.

Part 3 · Practice

What counts as usable material?

Not all raw material is equal. The lines worth building on are specific, a real moment, a real detail, not a summary. Decide which of these you could actually build an essay on. Pick for each, then Check.

"My grandma was really nice and we had a good time."

Too vague. "Nice" and "a good time" are summaries. What did she actually do? The essay is in the specific thing.

"She kept the hardest crossword of the week so we could do it together on the porch."

Specific, keep it. One concrete habit tells us more about her, and about you, than a paragraph of "she was nice."

"It was a really hard year for my family and we went through a lot."

Too vague, for now. There may be a real essay in that year, but "went through a lot" hides it. Freewrite toward one moment from that year.

"The morning we packed up his room, my dad folded every shirt like it still mattered."

Specific, keep it. One image (folding the shirts "like it still mattered") carries real feeling without announcing it. That's material.

Invention

The classical name for the generating stage of writing, coming up with material and ideas before you worry about order or polish. It's a real, separate job. Most blank-page panic comes from trying to invent, organize, and edit all at once. Do them one at a time, and invention comes first.

Freewriting

Writing fast and continuously without stopping to judge, from Peter Elbow and Ken Macrorie. Keeping your hand moving keeps your inner editor from interrupting, so you generate more, and more honest, material. The rule is simple: don't stop. If you're stuck, write "I'm stuck" until something else comes.

The "heat"

The heat is the spot with the most life, the line that surprised you, embarrassed you, or won't let go. It's where your real subject usually hides. When a sentence gives you a small jolt as you write or reread it, that's heat. Follow it; that's where the essay is.

No heat: "My grandmother's house was a special place where I always felt loved."
Pleasant, but flat. Nothing pulls on you.

Heat: "At my grandmother's house I was allowed to be the kid I couldn't be at home."
Feel the jolt? There's a tension there, a whole story pressing up behind one sentence.

Looping

A freewriting technique: after a freewrite, find the single line with the most energy and freewrite again from that line. Each "loop" moves you from the obvious surface toward what you actually mean.

Watch one work:

Loop 1. I freewrite about my nervous first day at a new school. The hottest line turns out to be: I ate lunch in the bathroom so no one would see me sitting alone.

Loop 2. I start over from that line and land on: It wasn't being alone that scared me. It was being seen being alone.

Loop 3. Starting from that, I reach what the essay is really about: I still hide rather than let anyone watch me not belong.

Three loops moved me from "a nervous first day" to my actual subject. That's the point.

Naming the feeling (affect labeling)

Research (Torre & Lieberman) finds that putting an emotion into words, "this makes me angry," "I feel ashamed here", actually settles the feeling and makes it easier to think about, not worse. For a writer, naming the charge on a memory is how you get a handle on it so you can write it well.

Getting it out of your head

Ramirez & Beilock (2011) found students who wrote out their worries before a high-pressure test did better on it, because offloading the mental clutter freed up working memory. Same principle here: getting the mess onto the page clears room to think and to write well.
