Reading Op-Eds Like a Writer

Your last writing project is the shortest and the most public. You'll take an argument and aim it at a real newspaper reader, someone busy, skeptical, and one thumb-scroll from leaving. (One project still comes after this: the digital portfolio, where you gather everything you've written.) This week you learn the form by taking it apart.

Project 3 begins. This week: study the genre and pitch your op-ed.

Good news

You already did the hard thinking in Project 2. The op-ed doesn't ask for new research; it asks you to make a version of your case land fast, for a stranger, in about 700 words. If anything, this project is a relief: shorter, punchier, and closer to how you actually talk.

Part 1 · Learn

Writing for the public is a different job

An (opinion editorial) is a short argument, usually 600 to 800 words, written for a , not a professor. That reader owes you nothing. She's scrolling, she's skeptical, and she'll leave the second she's bored. So public writing runs on a few rules the academic essay doesn't:

  • One idea, fast. No long wind-up. The reader learns your point in the first few lines.
  • A . A reason this matters right now, a vote, an event, a season, a headline.
  • Plain language. No jargon, no throat-clearing. If your smart aunt wouldn't say it, cut it.
  • Sources as , not citations. You hyperlink your evidence; there's no Works Cited page.
  • Your voice, loud. First person, personality, a point of view. This is the craft-infused argument at full strength.

The anatomy of an op-ed

Read enough of them and the same skeleton shows up. Here's a short one with its parts labeled, read it as a writer, noticing each move:

The Late Bus My District Wants to Cut Is a Ladder
The 5:40 activity bus smells like wet cleats and cold pizza. It's also the only reason I graduated.
This spring my school board will vote to cut it to save $90,000. For students who work or play sports and don't have a car or a parent free at three o'clock, that bus isn't a convenience. It's the difference between staying in school and slipping out of it.
EvidenceWhen districts cut late transportation, the sharpest attendance drops hit low-income students (state report).
Ninety thousand dollars is real money, and I don't envy a board that has to cut somewhere.
So cut it from the scoreboard, not from the ladder that gets kids to the game in the first place.

Notice how fast it moves: a vivid hook, the claim and the "why now" by the third sentence, one piece of linked evidence, a quick nod to the other side, and a closing line with a snap. That's the whole form.

Read op-eds like a writer. just means noticing how a piece works on you: who's the audience, what's the claim, where's the news peg, how does the writer use credibility, emotion, and logic ()? You can read excellent, free op-eds at The Conversation, where experts write for the public under an open license. Read three this week and watch for the skeleton above.

Part 3 · Practice

Name the move

Op-ed writers hit the same beats. For each line, which part of an op-ed is it doing? Commit, then Check.

"The first time I skipped a meal to make rent, I was nineteen and too proud to call it what it was."

Hook. A vivid, specific first line that pulls you in and makes you want the next sentence. It doesn't state the argument yet, it earns your attention.

"As the state votes next week on cutting campus food aid, colleges should protect one thing: the free pantry that keeps students like me enrolled."

Nut graf. It packs the claim and the news peg ("votes next week") into one sentence, telling the reader exactly what this piece argues and why now.

"Yes, a pantry costs money, and every line of a college budget is somebody's priority."

Counter. A quick, fair nod to the other side. In an op-ed you don't dwell on it; you grant it a sentence, then answer it.

"A college that can fund a new scoreboard can afford to keep its students fed."

Kicker. The closing snap, short, pointed, and it leaves the reader with the argument ringing. Great op-eds land the last line like a punch.

Op-ed

Short for "opposite the editorial page," where newspapers historically ran outside voices. It's a brief, argument-driven opinion piece (usually 600 to 800 words) written for the general public. Example: a nurse arguing, in her local paper, that the city should fund a second ambulance, using her own night shifts as evidence. One clear opinion, made vivid and fast.

A public audience

Not your professor, not other experts, but a general reader who is busy, skeptical, and free to leave. Example: the person half-reading their phone on a lunch break. Writing for them means no jargon, no assumed background, and a reason to keep going in every paragraph. You earn each sentence of their attention.

News peg (timeliness)

The hook to right now that makes an editor run your piece this week instead of never. Example: "As the council votes Tuesday on the bike-lane budget…" ties your evergreen opinion to a live event. No peg? Create a real occasion, a season ("as students move in this fall"), an anniversary, a new report. Timeliness is why op-eds get published.

Hyperlinked citation

In public writing you don't use MLA; you link. When you state a fact from a source, you make the key words a hyperlink to it. Example: "attendance dropped sharply after the cut" links straight to the report. It keeps the prose clean while letting a curious reader check your evidence in one click. No Works Cited page needed.

Headline

The title that earns the click. Strong op-ed headlines are specific and carry a hint of the argument or a bit of surprise. Example: "Ban Cars from Downtown" is fine; "I Sold My Car and Got My City Back" is better, it's concrete and it hooks. Editors often rewrite headlines, but a sharp one shows you understand the audience.

Hook (the lede)

The opening line or two, built to make a scrolling reader stop. Usually a vivid scene, a surprising fact, or a sharp confession, not a summary of your argument. Example: "The first time I skipped a meal to make rent, I was nineteen." It creates a small itch the reader needs the next sentence to scratch.

Nut graf

Journalists' term for the sentence or two that tells the reader, early, what the piece is about and why it matters now, the "nut" of it. Example: "As the board votes next week, it should protect the late bus, because for students like me it's the difference between graduating and dropping out." Claim plus news peg, in one breath. After a great hook, the nut graf makes the promise.

Counter (in an op-ed)

The same fairness move from your argument essay, but compressed to a sentence or two. You grant the other side's strongest point, then answer it, showing you're reasonable without slowing down. Example: "A pantry costs money, and every budget line is someone's priority, but a student who can't eat can't learn." Concede, pivot, keep moving.

Kicker

The closing line, the snap the reader walks away with. Short, pointed, often circling back to the hook's image. Example, after opening on a late bus: "So cut it from the scoreboard, not the ladder." A weak op-ed trails off; a strong one lands its last sentence like a door closing.

Rhetorical analysis

Reading a text to see how it persuades, the machinery under the words. You ask: who's the audience, what's the claim, what evidence, and how does the writer use credibility, emotion, and logic? Example: noticing that an op-ed opens with a personal story (emotion) before citing a statistic (logic) is rhetorical analysis. Doing it to others' op-eds teaches you to build your own.

Ethos, pathos, logos (recap)

Aristotle's three appeals: ethos (why you're credible), pathos (feeling), logos (logic and evidence). Op-eds lean hard on all three, fast. Example: "As an ER nurse (ethos), I've held the hand of a man who waited 40 minutes for a second ambulance (pathos); our response times rank last in the county (logos)." Three appeals, three clauses.
