The Transfer Drill

First you catch the problems in a stranger's essay, where it's easy, because you're not attached. Then you turn that same eye on your own draft. That distance is the whole trick.

Part 1 · Fix a stranger's essay

Here's a weak first draft. Read it, then diagnose three of its lines.

A meaningful experience that changed me was when I made the varsity team. Ever since I was little, I loved basketball. Tryouts were really stressful and I was very nervous. When I finally saw my name on the list, I was so happy. It just goes to show that hard work always pays off in the end.

"A meaningful experience that changed me was when I made the varsity team."

Throat-clearing. It announces the topic instead of starting in a moment. A real opening would drop us into tryouts, the squeak of the gym, the list on the wall.

"Tryouts were really stressful and I was very nervous."

Telling. "Stressful" and "nervous" name the feelings. Show them: hands sweating on the ball, reading the roster three times before the names made sense.

"It just goes to show that hard work always pays off in the end."

Tidy bow. It's a slogan, not a discovery. Did hard work pay off, or did you also get lucky in a good tryout? The honest, unfinished thought is stronger.
You just spotted a throat-clearing opener, a told feeling, and a tidy bow, in someone else's writing. Now here's the real test: the same three live in most first drafts, including yours. Let's look.
