RC 1000 · Week 15 · Module

Revise & Reflect

Almost there. This week you take your strongest piece one last step, and you write the reflection that turns a folder of essays into a portfolio: a short introduction on who you've become as a writer.

A lighter week over the holiday. Polish, then reflect. No new drafting.

Before you start

Looking back over a semester of your own writing can bring up a lot, pride, a wince or two, surprise at how far you've come. All of it is welcome. This week is gentle: read yourself with kindness and curiosity, the way you'd read a friend.

Part 1 · Learn

The reflective introduction

Every real portfolio opens with a : a short piece where you step back and say what your work adds up to and how you grew to make it. It's the writer's version of a gallery guide, it tells your reader (and me) what to notice, and what each piece cost and taught you. It's usually the last thing you write and the first thing they read.

Why reflection is the thing that makes it stick

This isn't busywork. Kathleen Blake Yancey, who shaped how writing is taught, showed that writers grow fastest when they stop and name what they did and why, a habit called (thinking about your own thinking). Naming "I learned to frame a quote instead of dropping it" is what turns a one-time fix into a skill you'll still have in your next class, and your next job. Reflection is how a semester becomes something you keep.

One more revision, on your best piece

You won't re-revise everything this week. Pick the one piece you're proudest of (or the one with the most potential) and take it further: run it back through the Revision X-Ray or the Editor's Passes, and make it as strong as you can. Give the other two a quick proofread. Quality over frenzy; this is a holiday week.

Part 3 · Practice

Real reflection, or empty reflection?

Reflection is only useful when it's specific. "I learned a lot" tells no one anything. For each statement, decide: does it show real, specific reflection, or is it a vague evaluation? Commit, then Check.

"I learned a lot in this class and I definitely became a better writer this semester."

Empty. It's a rating, not a reflection. Better at what? Name the specific thing you can do now.

"I used to drop quotes in with no set-up. The Revision X-Ray showed me how naked that looks, so now I frame every source and explain it."

Real reflection. It names a specific old habit, what changed it, and the new skill. That's metacognition, and it proves the learning.

"This class was hard but pretty good overall, and I'm glad I took it."

Empty. It reviews the course like a Yelp rating. Reflection is about your writing: what shifted in how you do it?

"My personal essay taught me to open in a scene instead of announcing my topic, so when I wrote my op-ed I started with the smell of the late bus, not a thesis."

Real reflection. It shows a specific move carried from one project to another, exactly the kind of transfer that proves you own the skill now.

Part 4 · Do

Write your reflective introduction

A few honest paragraphs. Use the prompts that have heat for you, and reach for the specific every time, name the move, the piece, the moment it clicked.

  • How would you have described yourself as a writer back in August? How would you describe yourself now?
  • Name one specific thing you can do now that you couldn't before (a real move: scene, the quote sandwich, qualifying a claim, cutting a darling).
  • Which piece are you proudest of, and what does it show about you?
  • What surprised you, about writing, or about yourself?
  • What will you carry out of this class into your next one, or into work?
  • If it fits, connect back to your philosophy of writing: after a whole semester, what do you now believe writing can do?

Reflective introduction

A short piece at the front of a portfolio where the writer explains what the collection shows and how they grew making it. Example openings: "A year ago I thought good writing meant big words; this portfolio is proof I've learned otherwise." It guides the reader and, more importantly, makes you articulate your own progress. It's a staple of writing portfolios for exactly that reason.

Metacognition

Thinking about your own thinking, or here, being aware of how you write and why. Example: noticing "I always bury my real point in paragraph three, so now I move it up" is metacognition in action. Research in learning finds it's one of the strongest predictors of whether a skill transfers to a new situation. Naming what you learned is how you keep it.
