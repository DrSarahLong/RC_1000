RC 1000 · Week 4 · Module

The Shitty First Draft

You have your material and your point. Now write the whole essay, start to finish, and let it be bad on purpose. Getting it down and getting it good are two different jobs. This week is only the first one.

Set aside real time to draft. It's the biggest write of the unit, and the most freeing.

A reminder

Nobody writes a good draft first, not published authors, not me. So the pressure to "write it well" right now is a trap. Your only job this week is to get the whole thing out of your head and onto the page. Bad is allowed. Bad is the assignment.

Part 1 · Learn

Permission to write badly

The writer Anne Lamott gave this its famous name: the (you're reading her this week). Her point: you can't fix a blank page, so the first draft's only job is to exist. You pour it out; you make it good later. Trying to draft and perfect at the same time is why people freeze. So don't. Just get to the end.

Two things to lean on as you draft:

  • Write toward the heat. Start near a real moment, not "This essay is about…" or "Ever since I was little…" (that warm-up is , you can cut it later). Keep going until you reach the thing that matters, then keep going past it. Drafts often find their best material in the last paragraph.
  • When you catch yourself summarizing ("it was hard," "she was kind"), drop into the actual scene, the moment, the detail, and let the reader feel it.

See the moves in action. Your reference shelf for this whole unit is the Craft Toolkit, nine before/after models you can keep open while you draft. Two of them matter most this week:

Start in a scene (cut the throat-clearing):

BeforeAn experience that really shaped me was the year my family moved across the country.
AfterWe left Ohio with the back seat so full I rode the last four hours with a lamp in my lap, the cord wrapped twice around my wrist so it wouldn't slide.

Show and tell (scene first, then the meaning):

BeforeSaying goodbye to my best friend when she moved was one of the hardest things I've been through, and it made me realize how much she meant to me.
AfterWe stood in her empty driveway and neither of us knew what to do with our hands. She hugged me too fast, got in the car, and didn't look back. I understood then that she'd already left days ago; the car was just the last part to go.

Notice the second "after" does both jobs: it shows the driveway and the too-fast hug, then tells us the meaning in one line. The reflection grows out of the image instead of replacing it.

Why "bad first" actually works

Generating language and judging language use different mental modes, and running both at once jams you (that frozen, blank-page feeling). Let the words come out rough and make the judging wait. You'll have plenty of judgment to spend in revision, next week.

Part 3 · Practice

Telling, or showing?

The single biggest upgrade in a personal essay is trading summary for scene. Read each line and decide: is it telling (stating a conclusion) or showing (giving the moment and letting us feel it)? Commit, then Check.

"My dad was really stressed about money that year."

Telling. It states the conclusion. What did the stress look like? That's your scene.

"At the register, my dad read the receipt twice before he'd let us leave, moving his lips over the total."

Showing. We feel the money stress without being told the word "stressed." One gesture does the work.

"I was so nervous before the game."

Telling. "Nervous" names the feeling instead of showing it. Get us into the moment.

"In the locker room I tied and retied the same cleat until the lace was damp in my fingers."

Showing. The nervous energy is in the action. We're in the locker room with you.

You still need some telling, it's how you move through time and reflect. But when a moment matters, show it. Aim for at least one real scene in your draft.

Shitty first drafts

Anne Lamott's idea, and the title of your reading: let the first draft be bad on purpose. You can't improve a blank page, so a first draft's only job is to exist. Getting it down and getting it good are two different jobs; doing them at once is why people freeze. Pour it out now, fix it later.

Throat-clearing

The warm-up sentences writers put at the start before they get to the real thing, "This essay is about…," "Ever since I was little…," "Merriam-Webster defines…." They announce a topic instead of starting it. Your first strong sentence is usually the second or third one you wrote; find it and cut everything before it.

Show, don't (only) tell

"Telling" states a conclusion ("she was kind"). "Showing" gives the evidence, the moment, action, detail, and lets the reader conclude it ("she saved the last crossword for us"). You need some telling to move and reflect, but the moments that matter should be shown. When you catch a summary, ask: what's the scene underneath it?
