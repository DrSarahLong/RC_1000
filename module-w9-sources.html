RC 1000 · Week 9 · Module

Sources: Think Like a Researcher

Your experience is real evidence, but it's one data point. This week you widen the case: you go find credible outside sources, learn to tell the trustworthy ones from the junk, and take notes you can actually cite later.

Back from break. This week you build a small, solid set of sources for your argument.

Before you start

"Research" can sound like a wall to climb. It isn't. You already evaluate sources every day (you know a sketchy website when you see one). This week just makes that instinct deliberate. You don't need to be an expert; you need to be a careful reader.

Part 1 · Learn

Why bother with sources?

Remember last week's warning about ? Sources are the cure. Good outside evidence does three things for an argument grounded in the personal: it widens the case beyond your single story, it backs up what your experience suggests, and it lends you , the reader trusts a writer who did the homework. Your job isn't to bury your voice under quotes. It's to bring in a few strong sources that stand behind you.

Telling strong sources from weak ones: SIFT

The fastest, most reliable way to check a source is a four-move habit called , from researcher Mike Caulfield. You don't do all four every time; often the second one settles it.

S: Stop. Before you share or cite, pause. Do you even know what this source is?
I: Investigate the source. Who's behind it, and why should you trust them? Look them up , not on their own "About" page.
F: Find better coverage. Is this claim reported by more established, reliable sources? If only one sketchy site says it, be suspicious.
T: Trace claims to the original. Follow a statistic or quote back to where it actually came from. Reporting about a study can distort it.
The research behind "investigate the source"

Stanford researchers Wineburg and McGrew (2017) compared how students, historians, and professional fact-checkers judged websites. The fact-checkers were fastest and most accurate, because they : they left the page immediately to see what the rest of the web said about the source, instead of staying put and trusting how polished it looked. Slick design fools "reading vertically." Leaving to check doesn't.

Where to look (and where to be careful)

Start where the credible material already lives: your and Google Scholar surface articles, books, and reputable reporting, all free to you as a student. The open web has good sources too (government agencies, established news, expert organizations), but it also has anonymous listicles and content farms, so you SIFT harder there. When in doubt, a Belk librarian will help you find and vet sources; that's their whole job.

Stuck finding good sources? You have real help, use it. Three things built for exactly this:

  • The RC 1000 library research guide: the databases, search tips, and citation help chosen for this course.
  • Book a one-on-one consultation with a librarian: free, and the fastest way past a research wall.
  • The library's research tutorials: short, self-paced walkthroughs for building the skills on your own time.

Take notes you can actually use

The moment you find something useful, record where it came from, exact source, author, page or link. Skip this and you'll hit next week's citation step unable to remember whose words are whose, which is how happens.

Part 3 · Practice

Source triage

You're building an argument that high schools should start later. Here are five things you found. For each, decide: Strong (cite it), Verify first (promising, but check it), or Skip (not solid evidence). Commit, then Check.

A peer-reviewed study in a sleep-medicine journal, found through the Belk Library database, measuring test scores before and after a district moved start times later.

Strong. Peer-reviewed, on-topic, and it measures the exact thing you're claiming. This is the backbone of a case.

A blog post titled "10 Reasons School Should Start Later!!" with no named author, no sources, and ads covering half the screen.

Skip. No author, no evidence, built for clicks. Even if it agrees with you, citing it weakens your credibility instead of building it.

A policy statement from the American Academy of Pediatrics recommending later start times, with citations to the studies behind it.

Strong. A recognized medical body, stating a clear position and showing its evidence. Authoritative and easy to trace.

A news article in a major paper summarizing "a new study" on start times, written by a named reporter but not linking the study directly.

Verify first. Reputable, but it's reporting about research. Trace it to the original study (SIFT's T) and, ideally, cite that.

A Reddit thread where dozens of students complain about waking up at 6 a.m. for first period.

Skip, as evidence. It's real feeling but anonymous anecdote, exactly the "lots of people I know" trap. It can't prove your claim, though it might remind you of a scene to write.

Part 4 · Do

Build your working source set

Step 1: what do you need to find? Look at your claim and reasons. List two or three things you're currently asserting that would be stronger with outside evidence (a statistic, an expert, a study, a real-world example beyond you).

Hasty generalization (recap)

Drawing a big conclusion from too little evidence, like arguing about all students based only on yourself. Example: "My online class flopped, so online learning doesn't work." Outside sources are the fix: they let you claim something broader because you're standing on more than one experience.

Credibility (ethos)

How much a reader trusts you and your case. You build it by being fair, being accurate, and using sources a reasonable person would respect. Example: an essay that cites a pediatric medical association reads as more credible than one that cites an anonymous blog, even if both make the same point. Weak sources don't just fail to help; they actively lower a reader's trust in you.

SIFT (the four moves)

A quick source-checking method from Mike Caulfield: Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, Trace claims to the original. It replaces long checklists with fast habits. Example: you see a shocking stat on a site you've never heard of, you Stop, Investigate (search the site's name), and often discover in ten seconds that it's an advocacy group, not a neutral study. Two moves, done.

Lateral reading

Judging a source by leaving it, opening new tabs to see what other sites say about it, instead of staying on the page and reading its own claims about itself ("vertical reading"). Example: rather than trusting a group called the "Institute for Family Wellness" because its site looks professional, you search its name and find out who funds it and what its agenda is. It's how professional fact-checkers work.

Peer-reviewed

An article that other independent experts in the field reviewed and vetted before a journal published it. It isn't a guarantee of truth, but it's a strong signal of quality, real scrutiny happened. Example: a study in the journal Sleep went through peer review; a post on a wellness website did not. Library databases let you filter for peer-reviewed sources on purpose.

Belk Library databases

App State's library pays for access to research databases (academic articles, newspapers, e-books) that are hidden behind paywalls on the open web but free to you as a student. Example: searching a database like Academic Search Complete gives you vetted, citable articles instead of the random mix a plain web search returns. Start there for credible sources, and ask a librarian when you're stuck; helping you is literally their job.

Accidental plagiarism

Presenting someone else's words or ideas as your own without meaning to, usually because sloppy notes blurred whose words were whose. Example: you paste a source's sentence into your notes, forget it wasn't yours, and it lands in your draft uncited. The fix is boring and total: mark every note as quote, paraphrase, or your own thought, and record the source the instant you take it.
